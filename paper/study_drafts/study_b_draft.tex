\begin{abstract}
\noindent This is an internal, numbers-focused draft covering all Study B results to date. Study B asks whether a differentially private release of patient sex preserves or washes out the subgroup pneumothorax AUC gap established in Study A, on the frozen 90/10 predictions file, across a fixed epsilon sweep. The mechanism used to answer this question was reworked once during development: an initial aggregate-count-based design was discarded after a technical review found it did not actually produce privacy-protected data (Section~\ref{sec:mechanism-history}). The corrected mechanism --- per-record randomized response --- was itself only single-draw-evaluated at first; a subsequent 30-seed replication (Section~\ref{sec:replication}) found that the single canonical draw's headline claims about direction reliability and about epsilon=4--5 being stable were both artifacts of that one draw, not properties of those epsilon values. The replication-corrected finding is that the gap is fully reliable only at epsilon $\geq6$; below that, survival is probabilistic and, at epsilon $\leq1$, direction itself is unreliable roughly 10--30\% of the time. Two follow-ups from an advisor-style methodology review are also included: 95\% confidence intervals on the replication's per-epsilon survival rates (Section~\ref{sec:replication}), and a post-processing debiased-estimator variant tried in response to the question of whether other DP mechanisms might do better (Section~\ref{sec:debiased}) --- it did not, a negative result reported in full rather than omitted. No narrative analysis beyond what is needed to interpret the numbers is included here; this is a results record, not a paper section.
\end{abstract}

\section{Methods (brief)}

\textbf{Input.} Study A's frozen \texttt{predictions\_90\_10.csv} and \texttt{patient\_split.csv} (test split only): 4{,}620 test patients, 2{,}470 male / 2{,}150 female, patient-level \texttt{true\_label}/\texttt{predicted\_score} aggregated by the same max/mean rule Study A uses. True gap (male AUC $-$ female AUC), recomputed directly from the frozen CSV as a cross-check against Study A's own reported value: 0.067036, matching Study A's canonical 0.0670.

\textbf{Mechanism.} \texttt{privatize\_categorical\_label} (\texttt{src/dp\_mechanisms.py}): diffprivlib's \texttt{Binary} mechanism, classic randomized response, applied independently to every test patient's true sex label. Each label is kept with probability $e^\epsilon/(1+e^\epsilon)$ and flipped otherwise --- a single, well-defined per-record epsilon-DP query, verified empirically to match that formula exactly.

\textbf{Epsilon sweep.} $\{0.1, 0.5, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10\}$ --- re-derived specifically for this mechanism's noise profile (a diagnostic found the transition zone at epsilon 3--4, comfortably inside the original six commonly-cited values, unlike the discarded mechanism, which never washed out the gap anywhere in that range).

\textbf{Test oracle.} At each epsilon, recompute the subgroup AUC gap using the per-record DP-protected sex labels directly (\texttt{true\_label}/\texttt{predicted\_score} unchanged). ``Survives'' = same direction and magnitude within 15\% of the true gap. ``Washed out'' = wrong direction or $>$15\% off. The 15\% threshold and the epsilon sweep are both fixed by project convention, not derived from this data.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Mechanism history: a discarded first version}
\label{sec:mechanism-history}

The first implementation released DP-noised aggregate male/female counts once per epsilon, then reconstructed a per-patient group assignment by randomly moving just enough patients between groups --- starting from their \emph{true} labels --- to match the noised aggregate target. A technical review before pushing found two disqualifying problems, not minor caveats:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Construct validity.} At epsilon=10, 0 of 4{,}620 patients were ever reassigned; even at epsilon=0.1, only $\sim$7 were. The artifact scored for AUC was true labels with minimal random perturbation, not privacy-protected data --- the experiment could not have shown a different qualitative result across almost the entire sweep regardless of whether genuinely privacy-protected per-record data would preserve the audit.
\item \textbf{Privacy accounting.} The aggregate mechanism's ``full epsilon per category via parallel composition'' claim holds under add/remove adjacency, but the test cohort's membership is already public (\texttt{patient\_split.csv} is committed to git) --- the real threat model is attribute (substitute-one) privacy, under which that composition claim does not hold (one attribute flip changes two bin counts simultaneously, breaking parallel composition's precondition).
\end{enumerate}

All commits implementing this version were discarded (the branch had never been pushed). The replacement mechanism (randomized response, above) resolves both problems: every output label is a genuine per-record draw, and there is no aggregate count or cross-bin composition question at all.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{The replication changed the headline, not just the error bars.} Two claims that read cleanly off the single canonical draw (Table~\ref{tab:canonical-sweep}) do not hold up:

\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Direction is unreliable at low epsilon, not just magnitude.} The canonical draw showed \texttt{direction\_match=True} at every epsilon, including 0.1. Across 30 replicates, direction agreement is only 70\% at epsilon=0.1, 73\% at 0.5, 66--90\% through epsilon=1--2 (Table~\ref{tab:replication}). The single draw getting direction right at low epsilon was luck, not a property of that epsilon.
\item \emph{Epsilon=4 and 5 are not the stable points the canonical draw suggested.} The canonical draw reported \texttt{survived=True} with pct.\ diff.\ of 1.3\% and 0.3\% at epsilon=4 and 5 respectively --- numbers that look like a settled result. The replication shows only 90\% survival at each: 1 in 10 draws at those exact epsilon values does not survive. Epsilon=3 is a near-exact coin flip (50.0\% survival). Only epsilon $\geq6$ reaches 100\% survival across all 30 replicates.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Numeric agreement on the crossover value does not mean the underlying claims agree.} Both the canonical single draw and the replication-based definition land on crossover epsilon = 3.0. That agreement is coincidental at the level of a single summary statistic; it does not rescue the canonical draw's specific claims about epsilon=4 and 5, which the replication shows were overstated.

\textbf{What is robust.} The gap's \emph{direction} is essentially always preserved once epsilon $\geq2$ (96.7--100\% agreement), and magnitude is fully reliable (100\% survival, SD $\leq$0.002) at epsilon $\geq6$. This is a genuine, defensible finding: at privacy budgets on the looser end of what's commonly cited in practice, DP-protected sex labels reliably preserve this subgroup bias-audit conclusion on a cohort this size.

\textbf{What is not robust.} Anything in the epsilon=0.5--5 range. Survival rate climbs roughly monotonically with epsilon in that band (10\%, 6.7\%, 36.7\%, 50\%, 90\%, 90\% at 0.5/1/2/3/4/5) but is nowhere near deterministic until epsilon=6. A policy claim of the form ``DP survives at epsilon=$X$'' for any $X$ in this range should cite the replication's survival rate at that specific epsilon --- with its CI (Table~\ref{tab:replication}), not as a bare point estimate --- not a single-draw boolean.

\textbf{The 15\% tolerance band is narrower than the reference gap's own uncertainty.} The ``survives'' criterion measures each DP gap against a fixed reference, Study A's canonical 90/10 gap of 0.0670, treated throughout this document as ground truth. But that reference is itself a point estimate with a wide bootstrap CI --- Study A reports [0.0230, 0.1118] (\texttt{study\_a\_draft.tex}, Canonical oracle result) --- while the 15\% tolerance band used here is $[0.057, 0.077]$, a span roughly 5x narrower than Study A's own estimation uncertainty on the number being compared against. This does not invalidate the sweep: the reference is deliberately held fixed across every replicate draw so that the reported variance isolates the DP mechanism's noise specifically, not test-set sampling noise, and that isolation is the right design for this question. But a reader of the eventual policy paper should not take ``survives within 15\% of the true gap'' to mean the true gap itself is known to that precision --- it is not, and no claim here should imply otherwise.

\section{Debiased estimator: a negative result}
\label{sec:debiased}

An advisor-style review of this draft asked whether other DP mechanisms should be explored for better privacy/utility. Rather than reopening the mechanism search itself --- Section~\ref{sec:mechanism-history} already shows the risk of that path --- this tries a \emph{post-processing} improvement on top of the existing mechanism's own released output: since \texttt{privatize\_categorical\_label}'s flip probability $p = e^\epsilon/(1+e^\epsilon)$ is exactly known, the released per-record labels can in principle be reweighted to reduce the AUC gap's attenuation, at zero additional privacy cost (post-processing on already-released DP output is free under DP's post-processing immunity; it never re-reads \texttt{true\_sex}).

\textbf{Method.} For each privatized draw, invert the classic Warner/randomized-response formula on the draw's own \emph{aggregate} observed rate to estimate the true population majority-share, compute each patient's Bayesian posterior $P(\text{true}=\text{majority} \mid \text{observed label})$ from that estimate and $p$, and recompute the subgroup AUC gap via \texttt{sklearn.roc\_auc\_score(..., sample\_weight=posterior\_weight)} instead of hard-assigning subgroups from the raw privatized label. Scored from the \emph{same} privatized draw as the naive estimator (both come from one \texttt{privatize\_subgroups()} call per draw) --- a paired comparison, so any difference reflects the estimator, not draw-to-draw noise.

\textbf{Result: this did not help.} The debiased estimator is worse than or equal to the naive one at every epsilon below 5, sometimes substantially (epsilon=2: 36.7\% vs.\ 3.3\%). The two converge only once epsilon$\geq$5, where the naive estimator was already reliable. The crossover epsilon moves in the wrong direction (3.0 $\to$ 4.0).

\textbf{Why, in two compounding parts, neither a bug:}
\begin{enumerate}
\item The population-share correction divides by $(2p-1)$, which shrinks toward 0 as epsilon shrinks toward 0 ($p \to 0.5$) --- exactly where debiasing would matter most, the correction itself becomes high-variance and prone to clipping to 0 or 1, degrading rather than correcting the estimate.
\item More fundamentally, AUC is a pairwise/rank statistic, not a linear functional of per-record labels. Unlike debiasing a mean or a proportion, reweighting the \emph{instances} passed to \texttt{roc\_auc\_score} does not correctly reweight the \emph{pairs} the statistic is actually computed over. Because the posterior weight here takes only two values across the whole cohort (a function solely of each patient's own observed label), the weighted statistic decomposes into a mixture of the observed-majority-only AUC, the observed-minority-only AUC, and cross-group pair terms --- not a clean debiased estimate of the true-majority-only AUC. A correct pairwise-level correction would need to reweight comparisons, not instances; that was judged out of scope here rather than attempted (see Limitations).
\end{enumerate}

This is reported as a genuine finding, not discarded: it is a direct, honest answer to whether an obvious utility-improving variant helps here. It does not. \texttt{privatize\_categorical\_label} with hard-assignment scoring (Sections~\ref{sec:mechanism-history}--\ref{sec:replication}) remains Study B's primary mechanism and result; nothing about the canonical sweep or its crossover changed.

\section{Limitations}
\begin{itemize}
\item Scope is the single 90/10 arm and Pneumothorax only, per Study B's fixed design (it does not sweep imbalance ratios or read Study A's other prediction files). Nothing here establishes whether this mechanism's reliability threshold would look different against a smaller true gap (e.g.\ Study A's 70/30 or 50/50 arms, whose gaps are 0.017--0.04 --- close enough to the evaluation noise floor that a 15\%-of-true-gap tolerance would be far tighter in absolute AUC terms).
\item The 15\% survival threshold and the epsilon sweep values are both fixed by project convention (CLAUDE.md), not derived from or validated against this data; no sensitivity analysis on the 15\% figure has been run.
\item No analytical or closed-form relationship between the randomized-response flip probability and expected AUC-gap attenuation has been derived --- every number in Table~\ref{tab:replication} is empirical simulation on one dataset realization. A theoretical treatment would explain \emph{why} epsilon=0.1 (flip probability $\approx$47.5\% kept) still retains a mean gap of 0.012 rather than washing fully to zero, and would generalize past this specific sweep.
\item Even 30 replicates is a finite sample: the reported survival rates (e.g.\ 90.0\% at epsilon=4/5) carry their own sampling uncertainty (binomial SE $\approx$5.5\% at $n=30$, $p=0.9$) that is not itself propagated into the crossover-epsilon claim.
\item Single dataset (NIH ChestX-ray14, via Study A's frozen output), single label (Pneumothorax), single mechanism family (randomized response). One post-processing variant (the debiased estimator, Section~\ref{sec:debiased}) was tried and did not help; no other local-DP construction or aggregate-release redesign was attempted. The debiased estimator's own failure mode --- reweighting instances doesn't correctly reweight the pairs an AUC is computed over --- points at what a working correction would need (a pairwise-level reweighting scheme), which was not built or tested.
\item The 15\% survival tolerance is measured against a fixed reference gap that is itself uncertain: Study A's bootstrap CI on the 90/10 gap is [0.0230, 0.1118], noticeably wider than the $\pm$15\% band ([0.057, 0.077]) used to score every DP draw against it (see Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). The sweep's design correctly isolates DP-mechanism noise from this by holding the reference fixed, but the reference's own imprecision is not itself propagated into any reported number here.
\end{itemize}

\section{Implications for the policy paper}
\label{sec:policy-implications}

\texttt{paper/Final\_Policy\_Recommendation.tex} is untouched until all three studies finish (per CLAUDE.md), but its current draft already stakes out language this study bears on directly, so it is recorded here now rather than re-derived later. This section is Study B's own read of its policy relevance, not a numbers record --- keep that distinction in mind when this material moves into the final paper.

\textbf{This is qualified support, not blanket support.} The policy draft's rebuttal to ``The Noise Ruins the Data's Research Value'' currently argues, citing an external review (Mohammadi et al.), that DP ``preserved clinically acceptable performance'' at ``moderate privacy settings.'' Study B can now back that claim with a project-specific result instead of a borrowed one: at epsilon$\geq$6, the subgroup AUC gap survives with 100\% reliability across 30 independent replicate draws (Table~\ref{tab:replication}) --- a bias audit run on DP-protected sex labels reaches the same conclusion as one run on the true labels, every time, on this cohort.

\textbf{But ``moderate'' is doing more work in the current draft than the data supports.} epsilon$\geq$6 is a loose privacy budget by most DP conventions ($e^6 \approx 400$), not the tighter range (epsilon$\approx$0.1--2) that discussions of ``moderate'' or ``meaningful'' DP protection often intend. Below epsilon$\approx$3, survival is a coin flip or worse; at epsilon$\leq$1, the audit does not just lose precision, it gets the \emph{direction} wrong roughly 10--30\% of the time (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}). If the policy paper's rebuttal is meant to cover commonly-cited protective budgets, this study's evidence does not support that as written --- the claim needs an explicit floor, not a general ``moderate settings work'' statement.

\textbf{This is also Study B's own instance of a risk the policy draft already names.} The ``Small Populations Are Harmed by the Noise'' section already footnotes that ``applied carelessly, differential privacy can even widen the subgroup performance gaps this paper is concerned with'' (Mohammadi et al., cited there). Study B's low-epsilon direction instability is a concrete, project-generated example of exactly that failure mode, not a hypothetical one anymore --- an institution choosing too low an epsilon, believing it more protective, could run a bias audit that reports the wrong-signed conclusion. That section can now cite this study directly instead of relying solely on the external citation.

\textbf{The debiased-estimator negative result (Section~\ref{sec:debiased}) closes off an easy escape hatch.} It would be convenient for the policy argument if researchers could simply correct for RR noise at low epsilon after the fact. This study tried the natural version of that and it did not work --- it was worse, not better, across most of the sweep. The paper should not imply that clever post-hoc analysis rescues low-epsilon utility; on this evidence, it doesn't, at least not for AUC-based subgroup audits via this mechanism.

\textbf{Recommended framing for the eventual policy text.} Replace an unqualified ``moderate privacy settings preserve utility'' claim with an explicit, scoped floor: something like ``reliable subgroup bias audits on cohorts of this size require epsilon roughly $\geq$6,'' attributed to this study, with the cohort size (4,620 patients, 2,150 in the minority group) stated alongside it rather than left implicit --- Study C's small-subgroup detection floor should be expected to push that number higher, not lower, for smaller cohorts, and the policy paper should not generalize this result past the scope it was measured in (single dataset, single label, single mechanism family, one 90/10 imbalance arm).
